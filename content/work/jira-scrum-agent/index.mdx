## The board is always slightly wrong

Every morning the team has a standup. Three questions, one at a time: what
did you work on yesterday, what's the plan today, anything blocking you.

In those fifteen minutes the team says everything the board needs to know.
Work moved. Something finished. Something new appeared. Someone is stuck on
a thing nobody else knew about.

Then the meeting ends, and updating JIRA to reflect any of it is a separate
chore that someone has to remember to do. Usually they half-do it. So the
board drifts away from reality, and by the time anyone looks at it to plan
something, it's describing a version of the sprint that stopped being true
several days ago.

The information was never missing. It just never made it from the meeting to
the board.

## Listening, then acting

The agent joins the standup and transcribes the speech. Then it works out
what the updates actually mean for the board, and makes the changes —
adding cards, removing ones that are done or no longer real, flagging the
blockers people mentioned out loud.

## Who said that

The part I liked most was also the simplest.

People join the standup from their work accounts — the same accounts they're
logged into JIRA with. So the agent doesn't have to work out who's speaking
from the audio, or ask, or guess from names. The meeting attendee and the
JIRA user are already the same identity, and it just uses it.

<Callout kind="decision" title="Use the identity that already exists">
Speaker identification from audio is a hard problem with a mediocre success
rate. Matching names to usernames is a guessing game that fails on nicknames
and people who share a first name. But the company had already solved this —
everyone in the meeting was authenticated, and that authentication was the
same one JIRA used. The hard version of the problem didn't need solving; it
needed noticing that it was already solved somewhere else.
</Callout>

## Where it got to

It was in testing when I left for New York to start my Masters, so I handed
it over to the development team rather than seeing it deployed.

That's the honest ending. It worked well enough to be tested, not long
enough for me to find out how it behaved with a team that hadn't been told
it was there.
