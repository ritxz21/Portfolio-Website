## Nobody wants to write the documentation

In a large company every project produces a PowerBI report — visualisations,
a data model, the numbers you put in front of stakeholders. For a serious
project that report is not small. The data models are enormous, with
hundreds to thousands of measures in them.

Two things follow, and both are expensive.

**Handover is slow.** Someone new takes over a report and has to work out
what a thousand measures mean, one at a time. Even for someone already on
the project, answering a passing question means going back into the model
and hunting.

**And the documentation is written three times.** A technical reader wants
the DAX and the dependencies. A user wants to know what the numbers mean. An
executive wants the summary. Those are three genuinely different documents,
so somebody writes three genuinely different documents — by hand, for every
project. It's a lot of time spent producing something nobody enjoys writing
and everybody needs.

## How I ended up on it

I joined and there was no development project waiting for me. What was
available was PowerBI analysis reports, which is real work but not the work
I wanted to be doing.

So my manager suggested I go and ask around — he gave me a few names of
senior people who might have something. One of them, a senior data
scientist, had just started an innovation project in the data department
with one other data scientist. Two people, very early.

I happened to have the exact overlap it needed: knowledge graphs, Python
development, SQL, PowerBI and DAX. They were willing to consider me, but not
convinced — reasonably. They'd been there for years and I'd been there
days, with no track record to point at.

So I built prototypes of what they were describing in a day, on generated
data. That did the convincing, and I joined the project as its main
developer.

I'd rather record that honestly than pretend I was assigned to it. The
lesson I took wasn't "prove yourself" — it was that a working prototype
settles an argument about capability much faster than a conversation does.

## What we built

The tool is a generator with a deliberately short path through it.

You upload the report and see it on screen in an editable form, so anything
the tool got wrong or thin can be corrected before generating. You pick who
the document is for — technical, user, or executive — and generate. The
result can be read in the application, downloaded as Word or PDF, or pushed
straight into Confluence through a connector, which is where this
documentation actually has to live to be useful.

That last step matters more than it looks. A generated document that ends up
in someone's downloads folder hasn't replaced the manual process; it's just
moved it. Landing in Confluence is what makes it the real documentation
rather than a draft of it.

**Same model, three documents.** The point isn't that writing is automated —
it's that the three audiences stop being three separate writing jobs. One
source, three renderings, generated on demand.

## The agent

The second half is a bot I built start to finish, on my own.

It's an agentic AI that knows the documentation. You can ask it about the
measures in general or about one specific measure. You can ask it to explain
the data model. And you can ask it to edit parts of the documentation
directly, rather than generating the whole thing again to change a
paragraph.

<Callout kind="decision" title="Why a document you can talk to">
A generated document is still a document — you read it top to bottom to find
one definition. Most people arrive with a specific question, not a desire to
read. The agent turns the generated documentation from something you read
into something you query, which is how people actually use reference
material.
</Callout>

## Internal tool, external users

It started as an internal application — Atlas Copco teams documenting Atlas
Copco reports. It didn't stay that way. Some of the company's clients ended
up using it too, and the Atlas Copco team in Belgium brought in NASA, whose
IT operations lead I spoke with directly about what they needed.

That conversation changed how I thought about the work more than any
technical decision on the project did. Building for colleagues means you can
guess — you know roughly how they work, and you can ask across the room when
you don't. Building for an external client's IT operations means you can't
guess about anything. What they need has to be asked, written down, and
confirmed, because there is no room to lean across.

## Who did what

Three of us. One senior data scientist led the ideation, the other did all
the testing, and I was the main developer — I built the generator, and the
agent end to end.
